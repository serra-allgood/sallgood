# Software vs Capitalism
## Craft vs Production
Computer software, depending on how you reckon what constitutes as programming, has existed for a little over one hundred years. It has been ruthlessly adopted by the capitalist system in that time. And yet, truly passionate people have within at least the last fifty years pushed as hard as they can for the free-as-in-speech, and ultimately free-as-in-beer as well, distribution of software. All while somehow being blissfully unaware of how niche our expertise truly is. The freedom to read and modify your software is meaningless without education in what programming is. Because of how capitalism has claimed programming though, the main paths are not laid out for education but indoctrination.

In [Programming as Theory Building](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/%7Eremzi/Naur.pdf), [Peter Naur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Naur) argued that programming isn't at all about writing code. Code is just a byproduct of the real work. If I can do a terrible job of summing it up, programming is about developing a theory of understanding of the intersection between the real world and your program. Not just the interface, but moreover, the almost metaphysical space that your program encompasses within the real world. Sounds very fancy, but Naur also argued that a program "dies" when its original creators are replaced as if they were fungible with any other software engineer. The real output of programming, the shared, cognitive understanding of the domain space the team approached, exists within the collaborative efforts of that original team. The program was *crafted*, not just *produced*, by that understanding. A new team can't access that understanding, they have to develop their own, and thus can't successfully modify the previous program without damaging it in the process.

The distinction between craft and production is an important one. It is not an original thought to point out that capitalists seek to make individual workers replaceable, to reduce their bargaining power and streamline costs. To that end, technologies that reduce the barrier of code production tend to be what become popular in the industry. Ruby on Rails made scaffolding a SaaS product a breeze. React reduced friction with state management in the front end and promised a future of composable, interchangeable components. Tailwind turned careful application of the CSS specification into a shotgun of utility classes. All of these technologies make the assembly line production of code easier, and working in the web industry practically demands learning them. All of them avoid the hard work of the craft.
## Modification vs Rewrite
They say you shouldn't reinvent the wheel, but humanity has been doing literally just that since the initial invention because one kind of wheel is not sufficient for every wheel oriented problem in the real world. But rather than being defined by the physics of actual terrain, the true nature of the programming craft is why there are a dozen open source projects for almost any given common domain. Each maintainer brings their own understanding of the problem to the table, bringing something inherently unique.

That friction between the truth of programming and the motivations of capitalism is what destroys businesses. Software engineers cannot be fungible, yet entire teams are broken up during "restructuring," forcing individuals to relearn how to work together, let alone understand together. Combined with the demands of increasing scale, businesses cannot move faster enough because they keep tripping themselves up without knowing it.

The extension of the truth I've been belaboring though, I found somewhat surprising. Even if the original team never leaves a project, and is able to share their domain understanding with new team members as they join, all programs have an expiration date. The real world is not static, and neither are its problems. This forces the team to adjust their understanding, slowly moving away from the original fit-for-purpose program they crafted. I once worked on a small team that explained during onboarding that the initial cost of software is cheap, but grows at an alarming rate, particularly if the foundation of the software isn't solid. The sad truth is, given enough time, even a perfect program is going to be too costly to modify. There's a legitimate reason why every programmer has said, "Screw it, I'll rewrite it from scratch," more than once in their career.

People are not disposable. But code should be.
